Decision paths · no unverified inputs

Compare the choices without inventing a winner

The economically strongest choice depends on eligibility, after-tax proceeds, legally collectible rent, actual carrying costs, and the household’s willingness to move. Those inputs are not verified, so no score or property-specific dollar result is published.

Best decision today: preserve optionality

Do not commit to a 2040–2050 hold, rent, or sale forecast using 2026 values. Keep the estate, title, tax-basis, lease, bill, and inspection records together. At inheritance, test Prop 19 promptly if a genuine move to the inherited home is plausible; then compare verified after-tax cash flows. A theoretical tax saving is not, by itself, a reason to move. The current one-year occupancy window is time-sensitive. Prop 19 rules · Timeline.

Five practical scenarios

What each path requires

Option 1 · move to the inherited home

Live in the inherited home; retain the San Francisco property

Potential benefit: if the relationship, transferor’s principal-residence status, recipient’s move-in, exemption claim, and continued occupancy all qualify, Prop 19 may preserve part or all of a lower property-tax base.

Trade-offs: the inherited home is not a rental while occupied; the San Francisco property still needs an operating and tenant-rights plan. Tax relief may be outweighed by lost rent, moving costs, or maintenance.

Unknown: every eligibility and financial input. No tax savings are calculated.

BOE Prop 19 guidance · SF rental-law coverage.

Option 2 · keep living in San Francisco

Rent the inherited home; retain the San Francisco property

Potential benefit: two properties may produce income or retain long-term optionality, if legally and financially sustainable.

Trade-offs: if the inherited home does not become the recipient’s principal residence within the required period, the Prop 19 family-home exclusion is not available under current conditions; the property may be reassessed. Rent income is not profit: include vacancy, repairs, insurance, taxes, debt, compliance, management, and capital reserves.

Unknown: lawful achievable rent, existing SF lease terms, actual expenses, and reassessed values.

Prop 19 requirements · Operating-cost register.

Option 3 · sell the inherited home

Sell the inherited home; retain the San Francisco property

Potential benefit: converts an illiquid asset to cash and avoids future operating and capital costs on that home.

Trade-offs: net proceeds depend on actual sale consideration, debt payoff, closing and transfer taxes, income-tax basis, improvements, and any rental depreciation. If Prop 19 eligibility is relevant, check the move-in and claim rules before sale; the timing can affect the result.

Unknown: sale value, basis, legal costs, transfer-tax treatment, and income-tax liability.

IRS basis guidance · SF transfer-tax schedule.

Option 4 · move and simplify

Move to the inherited home; sell the San Francisco property

Potential benefit: reduces the number of properties to manage and may align the household with a qualifying family-home move.

Trade-offs: the SF sale can involve existing tenant protections, city transfer tax, an income-tax gain calculation, and a separate property-tax history. A sale cannot be modeled as an immediate market-rent reset or a tax-free transfer.

Unknown: whether the unit can be sold vacant, sale costs, net equity, qualifying home-sale exclusion, and Prop 19 treatment.

SF owner/relative move-in rules · IRS home-sale guidance.

Option 5 · sell both

Sell both properties and redeploy the proceeds

Potential benefit: maximum liquidity and fewer property-management obligations.

Trade-offs: two sale events mean two sets of pricing, debt payoff, transaction costs, tax basis, and timing decisions. Any gain exclusion or tenant-related sale assumption must be checked separately for each property.

Unknown: both sale values, basis records, debt, closing dates, and reinvestment plan. No combined net-proceeds estimate is supportable.

Current transfer-tax references · Basis and home-sale rules.

Current-law guardrail

Do not assume the SF unit is freely re-rentable

Current San Francisco rent rules are unit-specific. The 1.6% schedule applies only during its stated period and only to units covered by the applicable rent-increase rules; owner/relative move-in has additional requirements and tenant protections.

Decision impact: use the actual lease, unit history, notices, and Rent Board guidance before assigning any future rent or vacancy date.

Current SF rates · SF law coverage guide.

Common comparison method

Use the same evidence standard for every path

Hold or rent: annual cash contribution

Collected rent + other receipts − vacancy/credit loss − property tax − insurance − owner-paid utilities − routine repairs − capital reserve − management/compliance − debt service

Use enforceable lease terms and verified bills. A published tariff or allowable rent increase is not a property-specific bill or a market-rent estimate. See the cost register.

Sell: after-tax net proceeds

Sale consideration − debt payoff − transfer/recording taxes − closing costs − income taxes on gain

Use a defensible valuation, lender payoff, current local schedule, and separate tax-basis analysis. Inherited basis is generally tied to date-of-death fair market value, subject to exceptions. IRS Pub. 551.

Order of operations

Five gates before choosing a winner

  1. Title and relationship

    Confirm the recipient, exact ownership path, and any trust or probate terms. Court process reference.

  2. Prop 19 eligibility and move decision

    Determine the qualifying family relationship, transferor home status, one-year occupancy feasibility, and timely exemption claim. BOE guidance.

  3. SF tenant and occupancy constraints

    Identify which units are covered, occupied, exempt, or subject to just-cause protections before estimating rent or sale timing. SF City guide.

  4. Verified operating cost and basis

    Gather actual tax and utility bills, rent records, insurance and inspection evidence, debt, and tax basis. Cost and value worksheet.

  5. Compare after-tax outcomes

    Only after the first four gates can a hold/rent/sell calculation be ranked on a common time horizon. Resolve open flags.

Recommendation boundary: At the evidence available today, the responsible recommendation is a process—not “live,” “rent,” or “sell.” Reassess when inheritance timing and the documents are known; do not let a hypothetical Prop 19 saving override a genuine lifestyle decision without a complete after-tax comparison.
