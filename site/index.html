The one finding that drives everything

Proposition 19 repealed the old parent-child property tax exclusion. For a transfer that happens in the 2040s, the Cupertino house keeps today's low assessed value only if the transferee moves in and makes it their principal residence within one year — and even then only partially. Held as a rental, or if the occupancy test is missed, it is reassessed to full market value on the date of transfer.

Recommended plan

  1. Do not sell during life. A lifetime sale is taxed at a combined 37.1% marginal rate on the gain (20% federal + 3.8% NIIT + up to 13.3% California). Property held until death receives a step-up in basis under IRC &sect; 1014, so an heir who sells promptly at the date-of-death value owes roughly zero capital gains tax. On a $2,000,000 gain that difference is $742,000.
  2. At the transfer, sell the Cupertino house and reinvest the proceeds. Selling beats living in it at every appreciation rate tested, and it beats renting it out by an even wider margin. The break-even portfolio return is only 0.68% — meaning the market would have to return essentially nothing for keeping the house to win. To justify keeping it, the house would have to rent for $39,712 per month.
  3. Do not disturb the 94122 property. Its assessed value is $116,533 against a market value of $1,800,000 — a Proposition 13 lock-in worth $19,892 a year in avoided property tax. Keep the owner-occupancy (homeowners' exemption), keep the rental income, and take care that nothing done to it causes a change in ownership.
  4. If the heir wants to live in the Cupertino house anyway, claim the exclusion properly and on time. Occupy within one year, file the homeowners' exemption within one year, file BOE-19-P within three years. The value of that exclusion is $19,297 a year. But understand that the exclusion is conditional: it is lost if the heir stops occupying the home.
