Scenarios

Live in it, rent it out, or sell it — modelled side by side, plus the separate question of whether the current owner should sell during life.

Three ways to deal with the Cupertino house once it is inherited. All figures are nominal dollars at the year of transfer.

A — Live in it. Benefit = the rent the household would otherwise pay for equivalent housing (imputed rent). Cost = carrying costs, with property tax on the Prop 19 partial-exclusion assessed value.

B — Rent it out. Benefit = market rent less vacancy. Cost = carrying costs on a full market-value reassessment, plus income tax on the net after 27.5-year depreciation.

C — Sell and reinvest. Sale at date-of-death value triggers no capital gains tax because of the IRC § 1014 step-up. Proceeds are invested; only the dividend slice is taxed each year.

Why the comparison is clean. The household occupies one unit of the 94122 property for free in every scenario except A, where they move to Cupertino and the SF unit becomes available to rent. That housing benefit cancels out when each option is compared against C, so the comparison does not need an estimate of what the third SF unit would rent for. No value has been invented for it.

The second decision: sell during life, or hold until death?

This one belongs to the current owner, and it is worth more than any of the above. A lifetime sale is taxed on the gain; a post-death sale is not, because § 1014 resets the basis to the date-of-death value.

The Prop 13 lock-in created by holding past the transfer

If the heir lives in the house, the assessed value grows at 2.0% a year while the market value grows faster. The gap is value that is never taxed. This is the one genuine financial argument for keeping the property — and it accrues to whoever still owns the house decades later.
